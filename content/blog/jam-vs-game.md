These are small projects, created during a short time period (usually 48 hours), and conforming to a theme of some kind.

Sometimes, after submitting a particularly good entry, I think to myself: *I've done it, I've created a game!* Then I think what steps I would need to take in order to sell the game on Steam. Bug fixes, of course. More polish. Maybe a new music track. Oh yeah -and also about 4 more hours of content 🤪

The kicker is that not every jam game or prototype can reasonably be extended that far. In fact, I would claim that *most* jam projects cannot be. The success criteria of jam games and 'real' games are simply different. I've ran into this problem head-on, when attempting to transform a jam entry into a commercial release. 

In this article I will talk some "jam archetypes" which aren't suitable for future efforts.

## Mini Games

These entries could perhaps appear as a *mini game* within another game, but cannot stand on their own. Play-testers usually enjoy playing them, but usually don't ask to re-play them. For an example of my own, consider the fossil-smashing [fossil-fanatic](https://sirlich.itch.io/fossil-fanatic).

And we put in some effort to avoid the association! We added multiple levels, a star system to encourage intentionality of player action, etc. But fundamentally it's still a minigame. Cleaning up the art, and adding 500 more levels wouldn't make it fit for steam. 





## The Content Question

Games need content. At least steam games do. I suppose for mobile the rules are different (flappy bird, etc), but for steam the challenge is to A) Deliver a game with enough content and B) keep the player engaged for the duration of that content.
